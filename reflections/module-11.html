Module 11 • Reflection Journal

Refining the Experience Without
Compromising the Argument

Balancing rhetorical intention, interaction, and accessibility.

November 2026 • 4 minute read

Prompt: What are some obstacles you are facing with your draft projects?

One of the most significant challenges I have encountered while developing my projects is determining when additional design work meaningfully improves the rhetorical experience and when it simply introduces unnecessary complexity. Throughout the development of The Elsinore Files, I have worked to transform Shakespeare’s Hamlet into an interactive investigation that asks participants to evaluate conflicting evidence, question source authority, and consider how incomplete information shapes judgments about responsibility. The technical challenge has not simply been making the experience functional. It has been ensuring that every interactive element contributes to the argument rather than distracting from it.

The investigative structure creates a particular challenge because participants need enough guidance to understand how to navigate the experience without being directed toward a predetermined interpretation. If the interface provides too much explanation, it risks undermining the critical thinking the project is designed to encourage. Conversely, insufficient guidance can create confusion that interferes with meaningful engagement. Finding that balance has required me to reconsider how navigation, visual hierarchy, source presentation, and participant choices work together to communicate the central thesis.

Accessibility has also required deliberate attention. Features such as keyboard navigation, readable typography, transcripts, and clearly labeled interactive elements cannot be treated as additions after the design is complete. They influence how the experience is structured and how participants encounter information. The challenge is ensuring that accessibility and rhetorical effectiveness reinforce one another rather than treating them as competing design priorities.

As I transition into the social media case study, I anticipate a related challenge: distinguishing between content that attracts attention and content that produces meaningful persuasion. Green and Jenkins (2011) emphasize that audiences actively participate in the circulation and interpretation of media. Their concept of spreadability provides a useful framework for examining how organizations encourage audiences to distribute branded messages. However, I want to move beyond identifying successful engagement strategies and critically examine why audiences choose to participate and how that participation contributes to a brand’s credibility and economic value.

Ultimately, the challenge connecting these projects is maintaining a clear relationship between rhetorical purpose and design execution. Whether developing an interactive literary investigation or analyzing a social media campaign, I want the final product to demonstrate intentional communication rather than simply technical proficiency or visual appeal. This module has reinforced the importance of evaluating not only whether a design works but also what that design asks its audience to believe, question, or do.

Reference

Green, J., & Jenkins, H. (2011). Spreadable media: How audiences create value and meaning in a networked economy. In V. Nightingale (Ed.), The handbook of media audiences (pp. 109–127). Wiley-Blackwell.
