Module 12 • Audio, Video, and Digital Storytelling

When Design Becomes
Part of the Argument

Evaluating every multimodal choice by the rhetorical work it accomplishes.

November 2026 • 8 minute read

Prompt: What are a few things you have learned from your project drafts?

Working through the drafts of My Literacy, In Progress and The Elsinore Files has fundamentally changed how I evaluate the relationship between a composition's content and the experience it creates for its audience. Earlier in the semester, I tended to approach design as the means through which an argument would be presented. I now recognize that the design itself participates in constructing that argument. Navigation, visual hierarchy, sequencing, accessibility, and interaction are not simply decisions made after the writing is complete. They influence which information audiences encounter, how they interpret its significance, and whether they are encouraged to question the assumptions guiding their initial understanding. Perhaps the most important lesson from these drafts is that adding complexity does not automatically produce a more sophisticated composition. Every element has to justify its presence through the rhetorical work it accomplishes.

That distinction became particularly apparent while developing The Elsinore Files, my interactive reinterpretation of Shakespeare's Hamlet. The central argument of the project concerns how source authority, presentation, and missing context influence judgments about evidence and responsibility. Creating an interactive investigation offered opportunities that a conventional essay would not have provided, but those opportunities also introduced substantial design responsibilities. Allowing participants to examine conflicting records, reconsider interpretations, and move between different sources meant deciding how much information should be available at each stage and how the structure of the investigation might influence the conclusions participants reached. The project could not simply provide a collection of documents and assume that interactivity would produce critical engagement. Its structure needed to encourage participants to recognize how their own reasoning developed as they encountered competing accounts.

One of the most useful lessons emerged from Dr. Foster's feedback on my storyboarding proposal. She challenged me to explain not only what a particular frame or interaction would contain, but why that choice advanced my central argument. That distinction forced me to examine the relationship between design intentions and audience outcomes more carefully. A document might appear convincing because of its formatting, placement, or apparent institutional authority, but those features only contribute to the project if the participant has an opportunity to recognize and question their influence. Similarly, withholding information can create suspense, but suspense alone is not sufficient justification for controlling when evidence becomes available. The more important question is whether the sequence helps participants understand how incomplete information shapes judgment. Revising with that distinction in mind made the project more purposeful rather than simply more elaborate.

Jody Shipka's (2005) discussion of multimodal composition provides a useful framework for understanding this process. Shipka argues that composers should consider the purposes of their work alongside the materials, technologies, methods, and circumstances through which an audience experiences it. Her emphasis on accounting for rhetorical choices closely resembles the design rationale work I have completed throughout the semester. I have learned that explaining why a design decision exists is not an administrative task separate from composing. It is part of determining whether the decision actually supports the intended argument. This has also changed how I understand revision. Revising a multimodal project can mean reconsidering an entire interaction, adjusting the sequence of information, or removing a feature that creates more distraction than meaning. Those changes are more substantial than correcting language or improving visual consistency because they alter the conditions under which the audience encounters the composition.

The development of My Literacy, In Progress reinforced another lesson about the relationship between structure and audience agency. The website presents literacy as an ongoing process, yet its navigation, reflection archive, and visual patterns necessarily organize that process into a particular interpretation of growth. This creates a productive tension between allowing visitors to explore independently and guiding them toward a coherent understanding of the site's argument. The drafts taught me that audience choice is never entirely unrestricted because the designer determines which choices are available in the first place. A website may appear open and exploratory while still directing attention through its hierarchy, organization, and placement of information. Recognizing that tension helped me understand that responsible design does not eliminate authorial influence. It makes that influence intentional and sufficiently transparent for audiences to navigate thoughtfully.

Module 12's attention to sound and video has extended that understanding beyond written and visual composition. In analyzing Marine Corps recruiting advertisements, particularly Battle Up and Made For This, I considered how narration, music, editing, and the selection of actual Marines shape audience perceptions of institutional identity. The campaigns demonstrate that authenticity is not established solely through the words being spoken. The people selected to represent an institution, the experiences shown, and the emotional progression created through sound and imagery all influence credibility. Julian Treasure's (2014) discussion of vocal delivery reinforces this principle by demonstrating how pacing, volume, tone, and silence affect the way audiences interpret a message. These observations are relevant to my own projects because they remind me that every medium introduces both rhetorical possibilities and responsibilities. Audio or video should not be incorporated merely because a platform supports it. Its inclusion should meaningfully change what the audience can understand or experience.

Accessibility has become an equally important part of evaluating my drafts. In The Elsinore Files, considerations such as keyboard navigation, readable transcripts, and alternatives to visually dependent interactions are necessary for ensuring that the investigative experience remains available to different audiences. I have learned that accessibility decisions also reveal assumptions about how a project is supposed to work. If an argument depends entirely on a visual effect, a timed interaction, or a particular method of navigation, I have to consider whether that dependence is essential to the meaning or simply a limitation introduced by my design. Providing accessible alternatives requires thinking more carefully about what the audience actually needs to understand, which can strengthen the rhetorical clarity of the project itself.

Ultimately, the most significant lesson from my project drafts is that effective multimodal composition requires a willingness to question the design decisions that initially seem most compelling. A sophisticated interface, an engaging narrative, or an impressive technical feature has limited value if it does not advance the central argument. I have become more attentive to the relationship between what I want audiences to experience and what the structure of a project actually encourages them to do. That awareness has made revision a process of evaluating purpose rather than simply improving presentation. As these projects have developed, I have learned to think less about whether a feature can be implemented and more about what its presence contributes, what assumptions it introduces, and how it shapes the audience's opportunity to construct meaning.
