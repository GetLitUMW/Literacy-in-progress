Module 13 • Peer Review and Revision

What My Readers
Helped Me See

Learning when revision should clarify an experience—and when it should preserve what already works.

November 2026 • 5 minute read

Prompt: Share what you learned from your peer feedback review this week.

The most important thing I learned from this week’s peer review is that a project can successfully communicate its larger idea while still creating smaller moments of uncertainty that interfere with the audience’s experience. Both Adam and Jasey understood the purpose of The Elsinore Files. They recognized that the investigative structure asks readers to examine competing accounts, record their reasoning, and reconsider their first conclusions as new evidence appears. Their feedback showed me that the project’s central argument was visible. At the same time, both reviewers identified navigation and the distinction between evidence and interpretation as areas that needed greater clarity. That combination was especially useful because it helped me separate problems with the project’s concept from problems with how readers moved through and interpreted the interface.

Adam’s response helped me understand that functional navigation is not necessarily obvious navigation. The controls worked, but readers sometimes had to look for the next intended action. In an interactive investigation, that hesitation matters. The project is designed to make readers uncertain about the evidence, not uncertain about how to continue. If the next record or checkpoint is difficult to locate, the interface creates a kind of confusion that has nothing to do with the rhetorical question I want the audience to consider. Revising the primary buttons, labeling the next step more explicitly, and placing progression controls consistently at the end of each record taught me that usability can protect the intended complexity of an argument. Clear navigation does not make the investigation intellectually simpler; it removes an unnecessary obstacle so that readers can focus on evaluating the records.

Both reviewers also asked for a clearer visual distinction between evidence and interpretation. Their agreement made me realize that this was not a minor stylistic preference. It was directly connected to the project’s central claim about how presentation and context shape judgments. If an adapted source record, an editorial explanation, and a reflective prompt appear too similar, the design risks collapsing categories that the project asks readers to examine critically. I responded by giving evidence, source context, editorial notes, inference, and reflection more consistent labels and distinct but restrained visual treatments. This revision helped me see visual hierarchy as a form of ethical clarification. The interface should not tell readers which account is true, but it should help them recognize whether they are looking at a record, receiving contextual framing, or being invited to interpret what they have seen.

I also learned from what my peers did not ask me to change. Adam described the comparison workspace as the point where he reconsidered his initial interpretation, and both reviewers saw the notebook checkpoints as a strength. Jasey emphasized that the investigative approach allowed readers to form independent conclusions rather than receive a predetermined answer. That feedback confirmed that the project’s most important interactive features were accomplishing their purpose. Revision did not require adding more branches, questions, animations, or technical features. It required making the existing experience easier to read and navigate without weakening the reader’s autonomy. This was a useful reminder that peer review is not only a search for deficiencies. It can also identify the parts of a composition that should be protected from unnecessary revision.

The feedback also changed how I think about the relationship between reader independence and authorial guidance. Earlier in the project, I sometimes treated those ideas as if they were opposites: either the interface guided readers, or readers made their own choices. The peer responses showed me that thoughtful guidance can make independent interpretation more possible. Readers need to know what kind of material they are viewing, what actions are available, and how to preserve or revisit their reasoning. Providing that structure does not determine what they must believe about Hamlet, Claudius, Gertrude, Ophelia, or the reliability of any record. Instead, it gives them a clearer environment in which to notice contradictions and make their own judgments.

Ultimately, this peer review taught me to evaluate revision by asking what kind of uncertainty each design choice creates. The uncertainty surrounding evidence, responsibility, and incomplete knowledge belongs at the center of The Elsinore Files. Uncertainty about whether a button is clickable or whether a paragraph represents evidence or commentary does not. Adam and Jasey helped me see where the interface was competing with the argument and where it was already supporting it. My revisions therefore focused on making navigation more visible, separating evidence from interpretation, and preserving the notebook and comparison experiences that encouraged readers to reconsider their assumptions. The result is not a different project. It is a clearer version of the project I intended to make.

Reflection Snapshot

A brief map of what peer review revealed and how that evidence shaped revision.

Peer insight
The investigation and reader autonomy worked, while navigation and content categories needed greater clarity
Revision focus
More visible progression controls and a clearer distinction among evidence, context, and interpretation
Central lesson
Usability should remove accidental confusion so audiences can engage with the uncertainty that belongs to the argument
What stayed
The notebook checkpoints, comparison workspace, open-ended reasoning, and absence of predetermined conclusions
