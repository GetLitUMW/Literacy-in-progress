Module Five Reflection

Space Is Part
of the Message

Fall 2026 • 4 minute read

Before this module, I thought about empty space mostly as a design decision. It made a page feel cleaner, less crowded, and easier to read. What I had not really considered was that space itself communicates.

The design principle that stood out to me most this week was proximity. Williams (2004) explains that related elements should be grouped together because physical closeness implies a relationship. Separating elements communicates something too. It tells the reader that one idea, section, or piece of information is different from another.

That seems obvious now that I can name it, which may be the most interesting thing I learned this week.

While building Literacy, In Progress, I have already been making decisions about spacing. Reflection titles stay close to their descriptions. Individual modules are separated from one another. Navigation items are grouped together. On the Patterns page, related information is visually collected into sections rather than presented as one continuous block. I made many of those decisions because they “looked right,” but I did not necessarily understand what they were communicating.

Now I do.

Writer/Designer describes proximity as the relationships created by spacing between elements and explains that those relationships help readers understand a text. That changed the way I think about visual design. A reader begins interpreting a page before reading the words. Where I place something can tell the reader what belongs together, what is separate, what matters, and where to look next.

That also connects directly to what I have been learning about literacy throughout this course. Literacy is not limited to decoding words. We also read space, color, images, hierarchy, placement, and relationships between elements. Sometimes the absence of something is communicating just as much as the content itself.

Going forward, I want to stop treating spacing as something I adjust until a page “looks good.” I want to use proximity deliberately, asking what relationships the layout communicates before the reader ever begins reading.

Reference

Williams, R. (2004). The non-designer’s design book (2nd ed.). Peachpit Press.
