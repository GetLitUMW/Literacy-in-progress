When the Plan Becomes
the Experience

Reflecting on the first working draft of The Elsinore Files.

October 26, 2026 • 5 minute read

Moving The Elsinore Files from a proposal and storyboard into a working draft made me confront the difference between explaining an idea and making someone experience it. In the planning documents, I could say that readers would compare evidence and reconsider assumptions. Once the archive existed, those claims became design questions. What would readers actually compare? What would they remember about their first judgment? What would give them a reason to reconsider it? A functioning website gave me something concrete to examine, but it also made the limits of my plan more visible.

Dr. Foster’s feedback helped me recognize that an interesting feature needs an intellectual purpose. The comparison between Polonius’s observation plan and Ophelia’s account is one example. Polonius treats the encounter as an opportunity to explain Hamlet’s behavior. Ophelia experiences the consequences of being placed inside that experiment. Presenting their accounts together makes the unequal distribution of power and knowledge visible. Her perspective changes what the encounter means because she has something at stake beyond helping someone interpret Hamlet. That distinction pushed me to think more carefully about whose experiences the archive allows readers to take seriously.

The visual record raised a related problem. Its close view isolates Claudius’s reaction during the performance, while the wider view restores the surrounding scene. The reader can see how framing makes one movement appear more conclusive. However, the draft currently uses a labeled schematic rather than staged photography. That makes the change in framing clear, but it may not create the same persuasive pull as a believable image. The audio has a similar limitation. Browser narration makes the adapted accounts available to hear, but it cannot yet provide the deliberate pauses and character-specific delivery I imagined. These are choices to evaluate during revision because each medium should contribute something meaningful to interpretation.

The notebook is where I can most clearly see the connection between the project’s argument and its structure. Readers can preserve an initial claim, encounter conflicting material, and return to what they wrote. Keeping earlier entries matters because a final conclusion alone hides the reasoning that produced it. I also wanted readers to be able to retain a judgment with reasons. If the experience rewards changing an answer simply because new information appears, it risks teaching compliance with the sequence rather than careful consideration of evidence.

That leaves an important tension in the draft: I am asking readers to form their own interpretations inside an experience I have carefully arranged. Labels, suggested comparisons, and questions can support close reading, but they can also signal the answer I expect. My next revision needs to examine whether the guidance helps readers explain their reasoning or supplies too much of that reasoning for them. Preserving uncertainty requires more than including a question mark. It requires leaving room for a defensible response I did not anticipate.

Before treating the project as finished, I want a small voluntary pilot to test those assumptions. I will look for whether readers distinguish a claim from corroboration, address a conflicting record, and identify how presentation influenced credibility. Those explanations will be more useful than completion time or the number of records opened. I also need to check whether the source passages encourage an actual return to Shakespeare and whether the navigation remains clear on a smaller screen. I have a working draft now, but I do not yet have evidence that it produces the learning I intend. That is the next step: watching where readers make connections, where they need support, and where my design gets in their way.
