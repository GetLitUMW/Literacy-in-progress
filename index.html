Reflection Journal, Module One

What Does Literacy
Mean to Me?

Fall 2026 • 6 minute read

For most of my life, I would have defined literacy fairly simply: the ability to read and write well.

Reading came easily to me from a young age. One of my earliest school memories is being separated into reading groups in first grade. I was already an advanced reader, and I remember being frustrated with our reading textbook because I was bored. As I got older, I became a voracious reader. I worked my way through the Sweet Valley Twins and The Baby-Sitters Club books, including every special edition I could find, at what probably seemed like an alarming rate.

Because reading was so central to my understanding of literacy, I think I developed a fairly traditional idea of what being “literate” looked like.

Digital literacy did not become part of my life until adulthood. I remember AOL chat rooms, AIM away messages, and eventually MySpace. Looking back, those spaces required their own kind of literacy. Choosing the right away message could communicate something without directly saying it. Your MySpace Top 8 carried social meaning. We were learning how to interpret digital signals and social conventions at the same time the technology itself was developing.

Social media has complicated my understanding even further. Today, being literate also seems to require understanding how information reaches us. Algorithms increasingly determine what we see based on what we have already watched, clicked, liked, or searched for. That can make it incredibly easy to exist inside an echo chamber where our existing beliefs and interests are continually reinforced.

I am also realizing that I still carry some assumptions about literacy that I need to challenge. When I first saw that The Baby-Sitters Club had been adapted into graphic novels, my reaction was negative. I thought a series I loved had essentially been reduced to pictures and fewer words, something I associated with books for very young children. I am beginning to question whether that reaction says more about my own definition of literacy than it does about graphic novels.

At this point, literacy means more to me than simply being able to read words on a page. It is the ability to interpret, question, and create meaning across different forms of communication. I suspect that definition will continue changing throughout this course, which is exactly what I want this archive to document.

About this project

Why Build This?

Rather than treating each weekly reflection as an isolated assignment, I created this site as a living archive. My goal is to make my thinking visible over time. By the end of the semester, I want to be able to look back at where I started, identify assumptions that changed, notice recurring themes, and see whether my definition of literacy expanded.
